1. Theoretical Background & Foundations
Genetic algorithms are usually used to identify optimal solutions to complex problems. This can clearly be easily mapped to search methods, which are aiming toward a similar goal. Genetic algorithms can thus be used to search for solutions to multi-value problems where the closeness of any attempted solution to the actual solution (fitness) can be readily evaluated. In short, a population of possible solutions (chromosomes) is generated, and a fitness value for each chromosome is determined. This fitness is used to determine the likelihood that a given chromosome will survive to the next generation, or reproduce. Reproduction is done by applying crossover to two (or more) chromosomes, whereby features (genes) of each chromosome are combined together. Mutation is also applied, which involves making random changes to particular genes.

Genetic Algorithms (GAs) simulate Darwinian evolution to solve complex optimization problems. They iteratively refine a population of candidate solutions by mimicking biological processes: representation (encoding solutions), fitness (evaluating success), and selection (choosing parents for the next generation).

1.1 Representation (Encoding)
Representation defines how a candidate solution is structured within the computer’s memory. It acts as the "chromosome" or "genotype" of your solution.
Binary Representation: The most common approach. Solutions are encoded as a string of 1s and 0s. Best for: Problems requiring yes/no decisions or feature subset selections. 
Integer/Real-Valued Representation: Chromosomes are lists of integers or floating-point numbers. Best for: Continuous optimization problems, parameter tuning, or pathfinding (e.g., [latitude, longitude]).
Permutation Representation: Solutions are ordered sequences (e.g., [1, 3, 2, 4]). Best for: Ordering problems like the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) or scheduling.

1.2 Fitness Evaluation
The fitness function quantifies exactly how "good" a specific chromosome is at solving the target problem. It serves as the evolutionary environment that drives the search.
Objective: If you are trying to maximize a mathematical function (e.g., f(x) = x^2), the raw function can be your fitness value.
Penalties: Constraints can be enforced by subtracting penalty points from the raw score if a solution violates problem rules (e.g., exceeding a cargo weight limit).
Normalization: Often, raw fitness scores are normalized (scaled) to prevent ultra-fit individuals from dominating the population too quickly during early iterations.

1.3 Selection
Selection simulates "survival of the fittest". It picks the best chromosomes from the current population to act as parents for creating the next generation.
Roulette Wheel Selection: Individuals are assigned a slice of a virtual roulette wheel proportional to their fitness. Fitter individuals have a larger slice and a higher probability of being chosen, but weaker individuals still retain a small chance to mate.
Tournament Selection: A random subset (e.g., 5 individuals) is chosen from the population, and the one with the highest fitness is selected as a parent. This is highly efficient and easily parallelized.
Rank-Based Selection: Individuals are sorted by fitness, and selection probability is based on rank rather than absolute fitness values. This prevents early convergence when fitness scores are tightly clustered.

1.4 Reproduction (Offspring Generation)
After selection, the algorithm applies genetic operators to create the next generation:
Crossover (Recombination): Pairs of selected parents exchange genetic information to produce offspring. Common methods include single-point crossover (swapping gene sequences after a random index) or uniform crossover (mixing genes randomly from both parents).
Mutation: Randomly alters small parts of the offspring's genes (e.g., flipping a bit from 0 to 1). This introduces new diversity and helps the algorithm escape local optima.
